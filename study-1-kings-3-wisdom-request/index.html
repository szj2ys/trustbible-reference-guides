How to Study Solomon’s Request for Wisdom

The chapter makes wisdom visible through a disputed case rather than leaving it as an abstract compliment.

Read first: 1 Kings 3:3–28. Use a complete translation and keep the passage open while taking notes.

What this study is for

Connect the request for an understanding heart with the narrative’s concrete test of judgment.

Set the passage in context

Solomon’s prayer occurs at Gibeon early in his reign. The dream dialogue contrasts a request for personal gain with a request to govern a people. The story of two women and one surviving child follows the statement that Solomon received wisdom.

Before applying the text, identify the speaker, the audience, the literary form, and the emotional or narrative movement. These facts restrain a memorable line from carrying a meaning the surrounding passage does not support.

Observe before you interpret

  1. List what Solomon could have requested and what he does request.
  2. Notice the stated reason he needs an understanding heart.
  3. Track how the women’s claims and the king’s test produce public recognition.

Write down only what a careful reader can point to in the passage. Then write a separate “Interpretation” sentence and mark any part that depends on background knowledge or a comparison with another text.

Compare related passages

  • Exodus 18:13–27 for delegated judgment
  • James 1:5 for a later New Testament instruction

Cross-references are not proof that two passages say the same thing. Compare their speakers, settings, genres, and purposes. A later biblical writer may quote, echo, or reinterpret an earlier text; say which kind of relationship you can actually show.

A five-minute worksheet

  1. Write the request in one sentence.
  2. Describe how the following story demonstrates the request.
  3. Separate the value of mercy from the king’s strategic test.

Worked study note

A careful summary might say: “Solomon asks to discern between good and evil in governing. The following case shows his judgment, but the solution is also a strategic test that reveals the women’s responses. The narrative links wisdom to public responsibility and spiritual understanding rather than to personal cleverness alone.”

The note names what the passage says, what it implies, and where the study stops. That boundary is useful: a short answer with its evidence is more reliable than a long answer that hides its assumptions.

Questions the passage invites

  • Which detail changed your first impression of the passage?
  • What claim would become stronger if you read the full chapter rather than one verse?
  • Which modern application needs a second passage or a wider biblical argument before it can be stated confidently?

Common reading mistake

Do not treat the disputed-child story as a modern parenting or custody procedure. It functions within a narrated contest for royal discernment.

Self-check

  • The prayer’s purpose is stated.
  • The following narrative is connected to the request.
  • The difference between ancient narrative and modern practice is acknowledged.
  • The note separates quotation, observation, interpretation, and application.
  • Any claim about “the Bible says” names the passage and translation being used.

Read with the citations in view.

Open TrustBible to read 1 Kings 3:3–28 with cited Scripture. Open the cited text, compare it with your note, and keep the passage—not the summary—as the final authority for what you claim.
