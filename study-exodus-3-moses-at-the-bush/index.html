How to Study Moses at the Burning Bush

The scene moves through interruption, identity, resistance, and promise; a useful study tracks each turn in the conversation.

Read first: Exodus 3:1–15. Use a complete translation and keep the passage open while taking notes.

What this study is for

Follow the dialogue from Moses’s ordinary work to a commission, objection, sign, and revelation of the divine name.

Set the passage in context

Exodus 1–2 establishes Israel’s oppression and Moses’s story. Exodus 3 begins with Moses herding his father-in-law’s flock. The call is not detached from the suffering described earlier; verse 7 makes the divine response to oppression explicit.

Before applying the text, identify the speaker, the audience, the literary form, and the emotional or narrative movement. These facts restrain a memorable line from carrying a meaning the surrounding passage does not support.

Observe before you interpret

  1. Mark each change in speaker and summarize the speaker’s claim in a few words.
  2. Notice what Moses asks and what the text says God gives him.
  3. Compare the place language—“holy ground,” “Egypt,” and “this mountain”—as the commission develops.

Write down only what a careful reader can point to in the passage. Then write a separate “Interpretation” sentence and mark any part that depends on background knowledge or a comparison with another text.

Compare related passages

  • Exodus 2:23–25 for the preceding cry
  • Exodus 6:2–13 for a later retelling

Cross-references are not proof that two passages say the same thing. Compare their speakers, settings, genres, and purposes. A later biblical writer may quote, echo, or reinterpret an earlier text; say which kind of relationship you can actually show.

A five-minute worksheet

  1. Map the dialogue into four movements: encounter, commission, objection, answer.
  2. Write what Moses wants to know about God.
  3. Separate the sign given to Moses from the result promised to Israel.

Worked study note

A dialogue map might note: “The encounter begins around ordinary shepherding. God identifies relation to Abraham and announces attention to Israel’s suffering. Moses resists by questioning his own adequacy and the name he should give. The answers locate authority in God’s presence and name. The final command broadens the scene from a local sacred place to a mission toward Egypt.”

The note names what the passage says, what it implies, and where the study stops. That boundary is useful: a short answer with its evidence is more reliable than a long answer that hides its assumptions.

Questions the passage invites

  • Which detail changed your first impression of the passage?
  • What claim would become stronger if you read the full chapter rather than one verse?
  • Which modern application needs a second passage or a wider biblical argument before it can be stated confidently?

Common reading mistake

Avoid treating every hesitation as proof of personal insecurity while ignoring Moses’s questions about authority and public reception.

Self-check

  • Speaker changes are tracked.
  • The preceding oppression shapes the interpretation.
  • The note distinguishes the sign from the promised outcome.
  • The note separates quotation, observation, interpretation, and application.
  • Any claim about “the Bible says” names the passage and translation being used.

Read with the citations in view.

Open TrustBible to read Exodus 3:1–15 with cited Scripture. Open the cited text, compare it with your note, and keep the passage—not the summary—as the final authority for what you claim.
