How to Study the Opening of Ruth

Ruth 1 creates an opening that the rest of the book must answer; it should not be read as though every problem is already solved.

Read first: Ruth 1:1–22. Use a complete translation and keep the passage open while taking notes.

What this study is for

Track the movement from famine and loss to return, loyalty, and unanswered need.

Set the passage in context

The story is set during the period of the judges. After a famine drives a family to Moab, the narrative reports deaths and decisions. The return to Bethlehem introduces grief, a public arrival, and the narrator’s final timing note about the barley harvest.

Before applying the text, identify the speaker, the audience, the literary form, and the emotional or narrative movement. These facts restrain a memorable line from carrying a meaning the surrounding passage does not support.

Observe before you interpret

  1. Trace every movement from one place to another.
  2. Mark who speaks and which names for the women appear.
  3. List the unresolved material at the end of the chapter.

Write down only what a careful reader can point to in the passage. Then write a separate “Interpretation” sentence and mark any part that depends on background knowledge or a comparison with another text.

Compare related passages

  • Ruth 2:1–23 for the next stage in the harvest setting
  • Judges 21:25 as a broader framing phrase

Cross-references are not proof that two passages say the same thing. Compare their speakers, settings, genres, and purposes. A later biblical writer may quote, echo, or reinterpret an earlier text; say which kind of relationship you can actually show.

A five-minute worksheet

  1. Draw the family line and mark where it breaks.
  2. Write one sentence about Naomi’s perspective and one about Ruth’s action.
  3. List the economic and social needs that remain at chapter end.

Worked study note

A sound note could say: “Famine, migration, death, and return structure chapter one. Ruth’s pledge to Naomi is an act of loyalty within a context of social vulnerability, not a complete solution to it. The ending emphasizes arrival and harvest, which creates expectation for provision in the next chapter without supplying it yet.”

The note names what the passage says, what it implies, and where the study stops. That boundary is useful: a short answer with its evidence is more reliable than a long answer that hides its assumptions.

Questions the passage invites

  • Which detail changed your first impression of the passage?
  • What claim would become stronger if you read the full chapter rather than one verse?
  • Which modern application needs a second passage or a wider biblical argument before it can be stated confidently?

Common reading mistake

Do not isolate Ruth’s pledge from the famine, widowhood, foreignness, and return that give it narrative weight.

Self-check

  • Movements are tracked.
  • The chapter’s unresolved needs are preserved.
  • Loyalty is connected to its social setting.
  • The note separates quotation, observation, interpretation, and application.
  • Any claim about “the Bible says” names the passage and translation being used.

Read with the citations in view.

Open TrustBible to read Ruth 1:1–22 with cited Scripture. Open the cited text, compare it with your note, and keep the passage—not the summary—as the final authority for what you claim.
