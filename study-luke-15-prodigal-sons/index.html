How to Study the Parable of the Two Sons in Luke 15

The parable begins with a demand for inheritance and ends with an older brother outside a celebration; the ending question matters.

Read first: Luke 15:11–32. Use a complete translation and keep the passage open while taking notes.

What this study is for

Read the story through the father, younger son, and older son rather than focusing on only one character.

Set the passage in context

Luke 15 contains three parables about seeking what is lost. The third story is the longest and ends without a recorded reply from the older son or resolution of his refusal to enter.

Before applying the text, identify the speaker, the audience, the literary form, and the emotional or narrative movement. These facts restrain a memorable line from carrying a meaning the surrounding passage does not support.

Observe before you interpret

  1. Track each son’s movement: away, return, inside, outside.
  2. List what the father says and does in both reconciliation scenes.
  3. Mark the unresolved final conversation.

Write down only what a careful reader can point to in the passage. Then write a separate “Interpretation” sentence and mark any part that depends on background knowledge or a comparison with another text.

Compare related passages

  • Deuteronomy 21:18–21 for the severe background that makes the father’s treatment striking
  • Luke 5:29–32 for eating with outsiders

Cross-references are not proof that two passages say the same thing. Compare their speakers, settings, genres, and purposes. A later biblical writer may quote, echo, or reinterpret an earlier text; say which kind of relationship you can actually show.

A five-minute worksheet

  1. Give each character a one-sentence summary.
  2. Compare the father’s speech to each son.
  3. Explain what remains open at the final verse.

Worked study note

A balanced reading might say: “The younger son leaves, wastes, returns, and receives a costly welcome. The older son stays near but refuses to enter the feast and complains that his fidelity was unseen. The father goes out to both sons. The parable ends with the father’s invitation still unanswered and the readers left to respond.”

The note names what the passage says, what it implies, and where the study stops. That boundary is useful: a short answer with its evidence is more reliable than a long answer that hides its assumptions.

Questions the passage invites

  • Which detail changed your first impression of the passage?
  • What claim would become stronger if you read the full chapter rather than one verse?
  • Which modern application needs a second passage or a wider biblical argument before it can be stated confidently?

Common reading mistake

Do not treat the older son only as a villain; his speech and the father’s response need to be heard.

Self-check

  • All three main characters are present.
  • The final unresolved invitation is retained.
  • The inheritance and celebration are not made purely metaphorical.
  • The note separates quotation, observation, interpretation, and application.
  • Any claim about “the Bible says” names the passage and translation being used.

Read with the citations in view.

Open TrustBible to read Luke 15:11–32 with cited Scripture. Open the cited text, compare it with your note, and keep the passage—not the summary—as the final authority for what you claim.
