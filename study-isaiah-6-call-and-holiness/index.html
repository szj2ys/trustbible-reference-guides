How to Study Isaiah’s Temple Vision

The chapter places a public political transition beside a temple vision and a hard commission.

Read first: Isaiah 6:1–13. Use a complete translation and keep the passage open while taking notes.

What this study is for

Trace the movement from the king’s death to holiness, confession, commission, and a message that will be resisted.

Set the passage in context

The opening date places the vision in a period of royal death and national uncertainty. The narrative moves from a heavenly scene to the prophet’s response and then to a commission whose outcome includes failure and judgment, before a final sign of remaining seed.

Before applying the text, identify the speaker, the audience, the literary form, and the emotional or narrative movement. These facts restrain a memorable line from carrying a meaning the surrounding passage does not support.

Observe before you interpret

  1. List what the prophet sees and hears in the temple scene.
  2. Mark the sequence of cleansing, question, willingness, and message.
  3. Notice how the chapter ends with judgment and a small note of survival.

Write down only what a careful reader can point to in the passage. Then write a separate “Interpretation” sentence and mark any part that depends on background knowledge or a comparison with another text.

Compare related passages

  • 2 Kings 15:1–7 for the king named in the heading
  • John 12:37–43 for one later New Testament interpretation

Cross-references are not proof that two passages say the same thing. Compare their speakers, settings, genres, and purposes. A later biblical writer may quote, echo, or reinterpret an earlier text; say which kind of relationship you can actually show.

A five-minute worksheet

  1. Map the vision by scene and speaker.
  2. Explain why the prophet’s first response is about speech and group identity.
  3. Write the full commission, including its difficult predicted outcome.

Worked study note

A careful note might say: “The vision begins with the death of a king and reveals royal holiness larger than that political moment. The prophet confesses as part of a people, receives cleansing, and volunteers for service. The assigned message includes resistance and exile-like judgment, ending with a sign of remaining life.”

The note names what the passage says, what it implies, and where the study stops. That boundary is useful: a short answer with its evidence is more reliable than a long answer that hides its assumptions.

Questions the passage invites

  • Which detail changed your first impression of the passage?
  • What claim would become stronger if you read the full chapter rather than one verse?
  • Which modern application needs a second passage or a wider biblical argument before it can be stated confidently?

Common reading mistake

Do not quote only “Here am I; send me” while omitting the message that follows.

Self-check

  • The full commission is included.
  • The political setting is not ignored.
  • The vision’s difficult ending remains visible.
  • The note separates quotation, observation, interpretation, and application.
  • Any claim about “the Bible says” names the passage and translation being used.

Read with the citations in view.

Open TrustBible to read Isaiah 6:1–13 with cited Scripture. Open the cited text, compare it with your note, and keep the passage—not the summary—as the final authority for what you claim.
